\begin{abstract}

	The goal of this report is to investigate whether federated learning (FL) over
	a weather station graph improves next-hour wind speed forecasting. The report
	uses completely independent local models as the baseline and compares them
	to the FL model using the weather station graphs. As the dataset, the report
	uses the Finnish Meteorological Institute (FMI) \cite{FMIOpenData} hourly
	observation data from 2024.

	For the FL models, the report constructs two graph topologies and compares
	them to the baseline model. The first graph topology is geographic k-NN which
	creates $k$ edges between the $k$ geographically closest nodes. The second
	topology is the correlation k-NN. In this graph, nodes have edges between their
	$k$ most highly correlated (similar) neighbors. Then the FL systems are trained
	with FedGD \cite{Jung2025} and compared with each other.

	After training the baseline and both FL models, the report finds that both FL
	systems achieve negligible improvement over the baseline, with the $\alpha$-sweep
	selecting a small regularization strength ($\alpha = 0.1$), indicating that
	strong collaboration is harmful. The result is explained by the local model being
	already well-specified. The local model has an abundance of data (one year) and
	a simple linear model leaves little room for graph regularization to improve
	forecasting.

\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
	Federated learning, networks, personalised machine learning, trustworthy artificial intelligence
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

Wind speed forecasting is valuable for grid operators managing Finland's growing
wind energy capacity. Wind energy consumption has been stably growing from year 2015
and in year 2024, the share of Finland energy consumption that was produced with wind
energy was 24\% \cite{StatisticsFinland2025}.

The Finnish Meteorological Institute (FMI) provides extensive data on their station
network publicly. They operate a dense network of stations across Finland and this
report tests the hypothesis of whether a collaborative federated learning (FL)
network might help create better wind speed predictions compared to local models.

Using federated learning is relevant for these types of weather prediction problems
because raw station data might be commercially sensitive or subject to data-residency
constraints thus ruling out centralized training. Another reason to use federated
learning is if the data is sparse or the sensors are prone to making
mistakes, local models can borrow predictive power from neighboring stations to
improve their own predictions. While personalized FL has been applied to wind
power forecasting over small wind farm networks \cite{LiWind2023},
graph-regularized approaches that exploit the spatial structure of dense
public meteorological networks for wind speed are still fairly unexplored.

The objective of this report is to create a federated model that predicts the next-hour
wind speed from the three previous hours. The report trains a local linear model
and then creates a FL graph that encodes which stations should influence each other
during training. The key design question in this report is whether the choice of the
graph topology (geographic vs. correlation-based) meaningfully affects the
forecasting accuracy.

Section 2 defines the FL problem, features, and graph topologies. Section 3 describes
the GTVMin objective and FedGD algorithm. Section 4 presents and discusses the results.
Section 5 concludes.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:pf}

In the FL graph, each node is one FMI weather station. All stations with less than
80\% hourly completeness over 2024 are excluded, leaving 165 stations. After feature
construction, 29 stations that lack data for one or more non-wind variables are also
excluded, leaving 136 stations to train the network on. The station distribution is also well divided geographically
over all of Finland (\cref{fig:topology}) so no geographical biases are introduced by
this filtering step.

Our local models forecast the next-hour wind speed at station $i$. Formally, the
prediction label is $y^{(i)}_t = ws^{(i)}_{t+1}$, the wind speed one hour ahead.
The next-hour forecasts enable grid operators to schedule dispatchable backup
generation and place intraday electricity marked bids before delivery. For
features, the report uses lagged wind speeds at times $t - 1, t - 2, t - 3$
and $t - 1$ lagged atmospheric pressure, temperature and humidity:

$$
	\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_t = \bigl[ws^{(i)}_{t-3},\ ws^{(i)}_{t-2},\ ws^{(i)}_{t-1},\ ta^{(i)}_{t-1},\ rh^{(i)}_{t-1},\ pa^{(i)}_{t-1}\bigr]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^6,
$$
where $ws$ (m/s) is wind speed, $ta$ (°C) air temperature, $rh$ (\%) relative
humidity, and $pa$ (hPa) atmospheric pressure. The lagged wind speeds are used
because they are commonly used as predictors due to the temporal autocorrelation.
In other words, consecutive time steps are correlated with each other. Pressure
gradients are what create surface wind thus justifying it as a feature. Temperature
and humidity don't directly cause wind, but they correlate with large scale weather
patterns like storms that affect wind speeds.

Both features and labels are z-score normalized ($\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$) using only
the training set statistics (so no validation or test data leaks into training). Feature normalization
ensures that no variable dominates the model gradient due to its scale difference
of physical units. Label normalization also removes each station's mean wind speed,
preventing the bias term to encode geographic location. For example, with label normalization,
two different stations from inland and seaside might have similar wind patterns, but
have different mean wind speeds. After normalizing, these stations can then theoretically
communicate in GTVMin \cite{SarcheshmehPour2023} (defined in \cref{sec:meth}) and improve each other's models.

The local model for each station is a simple linear regression:

$$
	\hat{y}^{(i)}_t = (\mathbf{w}^{(i)})^\top \tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}_t, \quad \mathbf{w}^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^7,
$$
where $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}_t = [\mathbf{x}^{(i)\top}_t, 1]^\top$ is the feature vector augmented with a bias term.

A linear model is chosen because only 6 features are used and each station
has abundant training data ($\geq 80\%$ of 1 year hourly observations). Because of
the massive amount of training data, no additional regularization is needed for
the linear models. The closed-form gradient of the linear model also pairs well
with the FedGD update rule.

The loss function is mean squared error (MSE):

\begin{equation}
	L_i(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{(i)}|} \sum_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \in \mathcal{D}^{(i)}} \left( (\mathbf{w}^{(i)})^\top \mathbf{x} - y \right)^2
	\label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}

MSE is chosen over alternatives such as mean absolute error (MAE) because MSE is
differentiable everywhere. This property of MSE pairs well with gradient-based
optimization and produces a clean and closed-form gradient for the linear models.
The quadratic penalty also penalizes larger prediction errors more heavily, which is
useful for capturing outliers in addition to typical measurements.

This report compares two methods for graph constructions. The first system is based
on station geography. The first graph construction method connects each station to its
$k = 5$ nearest neighbors. Connecting each station to its five closest neighbors
creates a small local neighborhood that share similar characteristics.
The distance between two stations is computed using the Haversine formula
\cite{Sinnott1984}, which accounts for the curvature of the Earth.

Edge weights in the Geographic k-NN system are assigned using a Gaussian kernel
applied to the pairwise Haversine distances:

\begin{equation}
	A_{ij} = \exp \left( \frac{-d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2} \right)
	\label{eq:geo-weights}
\end{equation}

where $d_{ij}$ is the Haversine distance between stations $i$ and $j$, and
$\sigma^2$ is the median squared pairwise distance (a data-adaptive bandwidth).

System 2 connects each station to its $k = 5$ most correlated neighbours. The
correlation is measured with Pearson correlation on 2024 wind speed series:

$$
	A_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t^{(i)} - \bar{y}^{(i)})(y_t^{(j)} - \bar{y}^{(j)})}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t^{(i)} - \bar{y}^{(i)})^2 \sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t^{(j)} - \bar{y}^{(j)})^2}}.
$$

Negative correlations are clipped so the code uses $A_{ij} = \max(0, A_{ij})$, since
GTVMin \cite{SarcheshmehPour2023} penalty requires non-negative edge weights. Even though the local linear models
use six features for forecasting, the station correlation is computed only using wind speeds.
This choice was made because stations that share similar wind speed behavior should have their
models drawn closer to each other. This graph construction method
should in theory be able to capture meteorological similarities beyond just geographical
proximity.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:meth}

The graph total variation (GTV) \cite{SarcheshmehPour2023} penalty uses the squared $\ell_2$ norm

\begin{equation}
	\|\mathbf{w}^{(i)} - \mathbf{w}^{(j)}\|^2
	\label{eq:gtv-penalty}
\end{equation}

to measure disagreement between neighboring stations' parameters. The squared $\ell_2$
norm is chosen over an $\ell_1$-based penalty for three reasons. First, it is smooth
and convex everywhere, giving a closed-form gradient that pairs directly with the FedGD
update rule. Second, it penalizes large disagreements quadratically, encouraging tight
parameter agreement rather than merely bounded spread. Third, adding a strictly convex
penalty to the sum of convex local losses makes the full GTVMin objective strongly
convex for $\alpha > 0$, guaranteeing a unique global minimizer.

With \cref{eq:mse} and \cref{eq:gtv-penalty} the full GTVMin objective is

\begin{equation}
	\min_{\mathbf{w}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{w}^{(n)}} \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} L_i(\mathbf{w}^{(i)})}_{\text{local fit}} + \alpha \underbrace{\sum_{{i,j} \in \mathcal{E}} A_{ij} \|\mathbf{w}^{(i)} - \mathbf{w}^{(j)}\|^2}_{\text{GTV penalty}}
	\label{eq:gtvmin}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is the collaboration strength. If $\alpha = 0$, there is no penalty
for differing models. If $\alpha = \infty$, all stations will share their model.
So raising the value of $\alpha$ encourages collaboration and decreasing it reduces
collaboration.

The GTVMin (\cref{eq:gtvmin}) is solved with synchronous federated gradient descent
(FedGD) \cite{Jung2025}. Using FedGD makes every node update simultaneously. During each iteration,
the weights are updated by

\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\mathbf{w}^{(i,t+1)} = \mathbf{w}^{(i,t)} - \eta \Bigl[
		\underbrace{\nabla L_i(\mathbf{w}^{(i,t)})}_{\text{local gradient}} \\
			+ 2\alpha \underbrace{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} A_{ij}\left(\mathbf{w}^{(i,t)} - \mathbf{w}^{(j,t)}\right)}_{\text{weighted disagreement}} \Bigr].
	\end{split}
	\label{eq:fedgd}
\end{equation*}

Each iteration, node $i$ broadcasts $\mathbf{w}^{(i,t)}$ to its neighbours and receives
theirs; all nodes then update simultaneously. The raw data $\mathcal{D}^{(i)}$ never
leaves node $i$ -- only model parameters are shared.

The following hyperparameters are used: All models are initialized with weights
drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.01)$ and trained for 750 iterations with learning rate
$\eta = 0.01$. For the $\alpha$-sweep, 300 iterations are used per candidate value.

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

As data, the report uses FMI Open Data \cite{FMIOpenData} hourly observations for the
full calendar year 2024, fetched via the WFS \texttt{fmi::observations::weather::hourly::simple}
stored query with a bounding box covering Finland (lat $59.5$--$70.2$°N, lon $19.0$--$31.6$°E).
The four variables used are hourly mean wind speed (\texttt{ws\_pt1h\_avg}), air temperature
(\texttt{ta\_pt1h\_avg}), relative humidity (\texttt{rh\_pt1h\_avg}), and atmospheric pressure
(\texttt{pa\_pt1h\_avg}). Stations with fewer than 80\% of the 8\,784 possible hourly
wind speed observations present are excluded, leaving 165 stations. After feature
construction and dropping rows with remaining missing values, 136 stations have
sufficient data and form the final FL graph.
Small gaps of up to two consecutive hours are filled by linear interpolation before
feature construction; larger gaps are left as missing and the affected rows dropped.

The data set is split into a training, validation and test split. With time series data,
the split has to retain chronological order so that future data is not informed by past
data. The report uses a 70/15/15 split: 70\% of the chronologically first
data points are in the train set, 15\% in the validation and 15\% in the test set.

Before comparing the performance of the two graph construction methods, an optimal
hyperparameter $\alpha$ has to be chosen. This is done by running the FedGD algorithm
for a fixed number of iterations and measuring the mean validation MSE across all
stations. From \cref{fig:alpha-sweep}, both graphs perform the best when $\alpha = 0.1$.
This value for $\alpha$ will then be used with both systems to compare their performance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The report tested whether federated learning over a geographic or correlation-based
graph improves next-hour wind speed forecasting. The report used a purely local model
as the baseline to research the effects of the federated models. When comparing the
FL models to the baseline, the report found that both FL systems achieved
negligible improvement over the baseline (\cref{tab:results}), indicating that
collaboration provided no practical benefit in this setting.

The $\alpha$-sweep selected a small regularization strength ($\alpha = 0.1$), which
suggests that strong inter-station collaboration is harmful. The boxplot
(\cref{fig:node-boxplot}) confirms the aggregate finding at the per-node level:
FL neither rescues the worst performing stations nor degrades the best performing ones.
The negative result is because of the simplicity of the local model. A 7-parameter
linear regression trained on abundant local data leaves little room for graph
regularization to help.

Several directions could extend this work:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{More expressive local models.} A shallow MLP or LSTM would introduce
	      non-linearities a linear model cannot capture, giving graph regularization more
	      room to improve performance.
	\item \textbf{Sparser data regimes.} With shorter records or higher missingness,
	      borrowing from neighbours becomes more valuable.
	\item \textbf{Wind-flow-aware graph construction.} A directed graph based on
	      prevailing wind directions could capture asymmetric weather-front propagation
	      that symmetric k-NN graphs miss.
\end{itemize}
